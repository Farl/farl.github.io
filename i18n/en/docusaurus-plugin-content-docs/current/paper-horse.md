## The story behind the model {#創作故事}

Before I was born, my grandfather was already making cellophane lanterns. He heated and bent bamboo strips into a frame, then carefully covered it with cellophane. Unlike the small handheld lanterns you usually find online today, his was a large battleship. It was remarkable. Before the Lantern Festival in 1993, my father used the same technique to make a rooster with wings as wide as an umbrella. As a child, I found it spectacular. I tried making my own lantern by punching holes in a can and putting a candle inside. I only did it once, but the idea that I could make lanterns myself had taken root.

I returned to the idea in 2005, the Year of the Rooster, and again in 2006, the Year of the Dog. For the rooster, I started a frame from strips of cardstock, but gave up halfway through. The following year, I made a dog lantern from corrugated cardboard and newspaper. It worked, but looked rough.

In 2007, the Year of the Pig, I changed my approach. Inspired by the shaped handheld lanterns at the annual festival, I designed a paper model lantern that people could print and assemble themselves. I was teaching children to draw at the time, so I wanted the construction to be simple enough for them to manage on their own.

After that, I began making a model for each zodiac year. Over time, I stopped calling them lanterns and simply called them paper models. I made rats, oxen, tigers, rabbits and dragons, and even a cat at a colleague’s request, although cats are not in the Chinese zodiac. In 2013, the Year of the Snake, I asked a printing company whether they could print the patterns on better paper. They proposed collaborating on Lantern Festival models to sell on their website. It became a rare and memorable experience.

Perhaps I had put too much into the snake. In 2014, the Year of the Horse, I could not work out how to represent the horse’s four long legs, and the tradition I had started came to a halt.

Fast-forward to 2024: someone asked whether I could share the old paper model files. That prompted me to revisit the collection and consider remaking the patterns at a higher resolution. Then came 2026, another Year of the Horse. The challenge I had left unfinished twelve years earlier drew me back.

For a model with a relatively simple shape and construction, the hard part is imagining the volume that will emerge when the paper is bent and glued. It takes repeated cycles of drawing, printing, cutting, assembling and revising before the shape comes together. Cutting out the pieces is the most tedious part. Every time, I am tempted to buy a laser machine to do it, although I now know laser cutting would scorch the paper’s edges.

This time, I also used Gemini. When a shape did not look quite right, I asked AI to edit an image as a reference. Its suggestions often went far beyond what I could actually make, but they still helped me find ways to improve the model. The elaborate acanthus decoration grew out of that process too. I doubt I would have come up with it entirely on my own.

There are still four zodiac animals left to make: the sheep, monkey, rooster and dog. The monkey will probably be another difficult challenge. I hope to work through them one by one. For now, please enjoy this paper horse for the 2026 Bingwu Year of the Horse. Happy New Year, and happy Lantern Festival!
